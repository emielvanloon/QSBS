QSBS Practical 4b — Regression diagnostics for GLMs Answers

Introduction

In Practical 3 we fitted generalised linear models and interpreted their output. In this practical we check whether those models are adequate for the data, following the four model-based diagnostic steps of Dormann (Sect. 9.1 and 10.1): influential points (Cook’s distance), dispersion, residuals, and the functional relationship between \(y\) and \(x\).

Exercise 1 — Influential points

In Practical 3 (Exercise 3) we modelled the proportion of contaminated wells in 20 areas on Long Island as a function of the nitrate concentration: glm(cbind(y, n - y) ~ nitrate, family = binomial, data = wells). Refit that model and call it fw.

  1. Calculate Cook’s distance for every area with cooks.distance(fw) and plot the values against the row number (type = "h"). Add horizontal reference lines at the two thresholds mentioned by Dormann (Sect. 9.1.2): 1 and \(4/n\). Which areas are flagged by each threshold?
  2. Look at the flagged area(s) in the data (wells[i, ]). Why does this particular area have such a large influence? Think about where it sits on the x-axis, how many wells it contributes, and how far its observed proportion is from the fitted curve.
  3. Refit the model without the most influential area (update(fw, data = wells[-i, ])). Put the coefficients and standard errors of the full and the reduced model side by side. By how much does the slope change? Does the conclusion about the effect of nitrate change?
  4. Draw the data (proportion y/n against nitrate, with point size proportional to n) and add the fitted curves of both models. Where do the two curves differ most, and why is that region exactly where you should be most careful with predictions?
  5. Write the sentence you would put in a report, following the two model sentences in Dormann Sect. 10.1.2. Explain why the influential area must not simply be dropped from the analysis.

Answer

(a) Cook’s distance combines the leverage of an observation (how unusual its \(x\) value is) with the size of its residual. It tells us how much all fitted values change when that one observation is left out.

The lenient \(4/n = 0.2\) rule additionally flags areas 2, 8, 17 and 19, but these are all below 1 and only marginally above 0.2. Area 4 is in a league of its own, and we concentrate on it.

Three things come together in area 4:

  • it has the highest nitrate concentration of all areas (7.0 mg/l, the next highest is 5.4), so it sits alone at the right end of the x-axis and has a large leverage: the slope of the logistic curve is largely determined by what happens at the extremes of \(x\);
  • it contributes 48 wells, so in a binomial model it carries a lot of weight;
  • yet only 2 of those 48 wells are contaminated (4 %), whereas the model, driven by the other areas, expects around 40 % at this nitrate level. Its residual is therefore huge.

Large leverage combined with a large residual is the recipe for a large Cook’s distance.

Without area 4 the slope more than doubles (from 0.34 to 0.82 per mg/l nitrate), and the intercept drops. The direction and the significance of the effect do not change (both models give \(p < 0.001\)), but the size of the effect is anything but robust: one area decides whether the odds of contamination increase by a factor \(e^{0.34} = 1.4\) or by a factor \(e^{0.82} = 2.3\) per mg/l.

The curves are almost identical up to about 4 mg/l, where most of the data are. They diverge strongly above 5 mg/l — exactly the region where area 4 is the only observation. Without it, nothing constrains the curve there, so the reduced model extrapolates steeply upwards. Predictions in a region that is supported by a single observation depend entirely on that observation, whichever model you pick.

(e) A report sentence in the style of Dormann:

“Area 4 (nitrate 7.0 mg/l, 2 of 48 wells contaminated) was identified as influential according to Cook’s distance (\(D = 36\), threshold 1: Cook and Weisberg 1982). Its exclusion did not change the direction or significance of the nitrate effect (\(p < 0.001\) in both cases), but the estimated slope increased from 0.34 (SE 0.06) to 0.82 (SE 0.10) per mg/l, so the size of the effect at high nitrate concentrations is uncertain. We report the model based on all areas.”

The area cannot simply be dropped because there is no indication that it is a data error: it is a real area with real measurements, and it happens to be the only information we have about high nitrate concentrations. Deleting it would mean fitting a model that claims to know what happens at 7 mg/l while having thrown away the only observation at that level. What we can do is show that the analysis is (or is not) robust to its exclusion, and say so — which is what the sentence above does.

Exercise 2 — Dispersion

In this exercise we look at over- and underdispersion, and at how a quasi-likelihood model corrects the standard errors.

  1. For the binomial model fw from Exercise 1, calculate the ratio residual deviance / residual degrees of freedom. Also calculate the Pearson version, \(\sum r_P^2 / \mathrm{df}\), with residuals(fw, type = "pearson"). Are the data over- or underdispersed? Is a binomial model with 20 areas on Long Island likely to satisfy the assumption that all wells in an area are independent draws with the same probability?
  2. Refit the model with family = quasibinomial and compare the summary() of the two fits. Which numbers are identical, which have changed, and what is the value reported as “Dispersion parameter”? Show that the standard errors of the quasi-model equal those of the binomial model multiplied by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\). What happens to the p-value of the nitrate effect?
  3. Now take the Mallard clutch-size data clutch (see ?clutch): the number of eggs (CLUTCH) in nests in artificial nesting structures, together with the Julian date on which the nest was found (date). Plot CLUTCH against date, fit a Poisson GLM CLUTCH ~ date and calculate the dispersion ratio. Are these data over- or underdispersed? Give a biological reason why clutch sizes might be less variable than a Poisson distribution predicts.
  4. Refit the clutch model with family = quasipoisson. In which direction do the standard errors move, and by what factor? What does this imply for the p-value and for the width of a confidence interval of the date effect? Explain in one sentence why underdispersion is, in a sense, a “luckier” problem to have than overdispersion.
  5. Finally, return to the Galápagos species–area model of Practical 3 (Exercise 4): glm(Species ~ log(Area), family = poisson, data = gala). The dispersion ratio is enormous. Fit a quasi-Poisson model and a negative binomial model (glm.nb()) and put the estimate and standard error of the log(Area) slope from all three models in one table. Which of the two remedies changes the estimate, and which one only changes the standard error? Why can the quasi-Poisson model not be compared with the others by AIC?

Answer

(a)

c(deviance_ratio = deviance(fw) / df.residual(fw),
  pearson_ratio  = sum(residuals(fw, type = "pearson")^2) / df.residual(fw))
deviance_ratio  pearson_ratio 
      6.231120       5.572557

Both ratios are about 6, far above the “\(> 2\)” rule of thumb: the proportions are strongly overdispersed. This is not surprising. Wells within one area are not independent draws with one common probability: they share local geology, depth, distance to sources of pollution, etc. Such extra-binomial variation between wells within an area — and between areas that are not captured by nitrate alone — inflates the variance beyond \(np(1-p)\).

(b)

Identical: the estimates, the deviances and the degrees of freedom — the quasi-likelihood uses the same score equations, so the fitted curve is unchanged. Changed: the standard errors, the test statistics (now \(t\) instead of \(z\)) and the p-values; the AIC is reported as NA. The “Dispersion parameter for quasibinomial family” is \(\hat\phi = 5.57\), which is the Pearson ratio from (a) — not the deviance ratio.

The standard errors are exactly the binomial ones multiplied by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\):

The p-value of the nitrate effect goes from \(8 \times 10^{-9}\) to \(0.025\): still significant, but the binomial model exaggerated the evidence by many orders of magnitude. That is what “underestimated standard errors” means in practice.

(c)

  pearson_ratio  = sum(residuals(fc, type = "pearson")^2) / df.residual(fc),
  mean = mean(clutch$CLUTCH), variance = var(clutch$CLUTCH))
deviance_ratio  pearson_ratio           mean       variance 
     0.4333727      0.5099544     11.2446043      6.6788656

The ratio is about 0.4–0.5, well below Dormann’s “\(< 0.6\)” limit: the clutch sizes are underdispersed. Already the raw numbers show it: the variance (6.7) is much smaller than the mean (11.2), whereas a Poisson variable would have both equal.

Biologically this is what we expect. A Poisson distribution describes counts of independent events; eggs in a clutch are not independent events but the outcome of a tightly regulated physiological process. A Mallard lays roughly 9–13 eggs; clutches of 2 or 25 are physiologically or evolutionarily “not allowed”. Regulation squeezes the variance below the Poisson value.

Now \(\hat\phi = 0.51 < 1\), so the standard errors shrink by a factor \(\sqrt{0.51} = 0.71\). The \(t\)-value of the date effect rises from 3.3 to 4.7, the p-value falls from \(9 \times 10^{-4}\) to \(7 \times 10^{-6}\), and a confidence interval for the slope becomes about 30 % narrower.

Underdispersion is the “luckier” problem because the naive Poisson model is conservative: its standard errors are too large, so whatever it declares significant is significant; correcting for the underdispersion can only strengthen the conclusions, whereas correcting for overdispersion can make an apparently clear effect disappear.

The quasi-Poisson model keeps the Poisson estimate (\(z = 0.343\)) and only inflates the standard error, from 0.007 to about 0.036 (a factor \(\sqrt{23.8} \approx 4.9\)). The negative binomial model changes the estimate as well (\(z = 0.367\)), because it weights the observations differently: the large islands with their huge counts no longer dominate the fit as they do under the Poisson variance. Its standard error is similar to the quasi-Poisson one.

The quasi-Poisson fit has no likelihood — only a quasi-likelihood, a set of estimating equations that is not the derivative of any probability model — so logLik() and hence AIC are undefined (AIC() returns NA). The Poisson and negative binomial models, in contrast, are proper probability models for the same data and can be compared by AIC: the negative binomial wins by more than 500 units.

Exercise 3 — Residuals

We return to the macro-fauna data RIKZdat and the Poisson model of species richness against tidal height, glm(Richness ~ NAP, family = poisson, data = RIKZdat), which we call fr.

  1. Calculate the standardised deviance residuals with rstandard(fr) and the fitted values on the link scale with predict(fr, type = "link"). Plot the residuals against the fitted values, add a horizontal line at 0 and a smoother (lines(lowess(...))). What should this plot look like if the model were adequate? What do you actually see?
  2. Why do we plot against the linear predictor \(\hat\eta\) rather than against the fitted means \(\hat y\) on the response scale? Make the same plot with predict(fr, type = "response") on the x-axis and describe how the picture changes (hint: look at the spacing of the points along the x-axis).
  3. How many residuals lie outside \(\pm 2\), and which stations are they (which(abs(rSD) > 2))? For standardised residuals roughly 5 % should exceed \(\pm 2\) in absolute value; is that what you find? Is any single station also flagged by Cook’s distance?
  4. Plot the residuals against the predictor NAP. Is there a systematic pattern, e.g. a curvature? What kind of model change would this suggest? (We will test it in Exercise 4.)
  5. The data set contains more variables than NAP. Plot the residuals against exposure (a variable with three values, so use a boxplot with factor(exposure)) and against humus. Is there structure left in the residuals that one of these variables could explain? Extend the model with the variable that shows the clearest pattern, refit, and check whether the dispersion ratio and the residual plots have improved.

Answer

(a) Standardised deviance residuals are deviance residuals divided by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi (1 - h_i)}\), so they are corrected for the variance function, for the dispersion and for the leverage \(h_i\) of each point. If the model is adequate they behave approximately like standard-normal noise: a horizontal, structureless band around 0, about 95 % of the points within \(\pm 2\), and no relation between their spread and the x-axis.

fr  <- glm(Richness ~ NAP, family = poisson, data = RIKZdat)
rSD <- rstandard(fr)
eta <- predict(fr, type = "link")

plot(eta, rSD, pch = 16, las = 1,
     xlab = expression(hat(eta) == log(hat(lambda))), ylab = "standardised deviance residual")
abline(h = 0, lty = 3)
abline(h = c(-2, 2), lty = 3, col = "grey60")
lines(lowess(eta, rSD), col = "firebrick", lwd = 2)

What we see instead: the residuals are far from a tidy band. There are several very large positive residuals (up to +5.5) and the spread is clearly larger for the higher fitted values — consistent with the overdispersion (deviance ratio 2.6). The smoother is not flat either: it lies below 0 at both ends of the range and rises to 0 in the middle, suggesting some curvature that a straight line on the log scale does not capture. Also note that the smoother sits mostly below zero: the median residual is negative, while the mean is pulled to 0 by the few very large positive residuals.

(b) The GLM assumes linearity on the link scale: \(\log\lambda = a + b\,\mathrm{NAP}\). Any misspecification of that linear predictor shows up most cleanly when we plot against \(\hat\eta\). On the response scale the inverse link (exp()) compresses small values together and stretches large ones apart, so the points are unevenly spaced and curvature is distorted.

yhat <- predict(fr, type = "response")

plot(yhat, rSD, pch = 16, las = 1,
     xlab = expression(hat(lambda)), ylab = "standardised deviance residual")
abline(h = 0, lty = 3)
lines(lowess(yhat, rSD), col = "firebrick", lwd = 2)

The residuals themselves are identical (they are the same numbers), but the x-axis is now exp(eta): the low-richness stations are squashed into the left part of the plot and the few high-richness stations are stretched out to the right, making the smoother less reliable there. Note that with only one predictor the plot against \(\hat\eta\) is, up to a linear rescaling of the x-axis, the same as the plot against NAP in (d).

Exercise 4 — The functional relationship between y and x

The residual plot in Exercise 3 hinted that a straight line on the log scale may not be the best description of how richness depends on NAP. We now compare the model fr with two alternatives.

  1. Draw the data and the fitted curve of fr with its 95 % confidence band, following the recipe from Practical 3: a fine sequence of NAP values, predict(..., se.fit = TRUE) on the link scale, \(\pm 2\) standard errors, and exp() before drawing. Use matlines() to draw the three lines in one go.
  2. Alternative 1 — a different error distribution. Fit glm.nb(Richness ~ NAP, data = RIKZdat). Both models describe the same curve: identical fitted values, identical deviance and AIC, and the test for the quadratic term gives the same \(z = -2.52\), \(p = 0.012\) in both. But the coefficients differ, because poly() builds two orthogonal (uncorrelated) columns, whereas NAP and NAP^2 are correlated. In the explicit model, the coefficient of NAP (\(-0.50\)) and its standard error refer to the slope at NAP = 0, and the estimate of the linear term changes when the quadratic term is added; the two estimates are correlated and their individual standard errors are inflated by that correlation. With poly() the linear coefficient stays what it was, and the test of the second term is a clean test of “is there curvature beyond the straight line”.

So: use poly() when the question is whether a polynomial term is needed (and always use predict() to draw it, never plug the coefficients into \(a + bx + cx^2\)). Use the explicit form if you want to read off the curve: \(\log\lambda = 1.94 - 0.50\,\mathrm{NAP} - 0.20\,\mathrm{NAP}^2\), with its maximum at \(\mathrm{NAP} = -b/(2c) = -0.50 / (2 \times 0.20) \approx -1.25\) m.
