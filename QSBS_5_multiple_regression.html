QSBS Practical 5 — Multiple regression: more than one predictor

Introduction

Up to now every regression model had a single predictor. In this practical we add more predictors (Dormann, Chap. 15 and 16). In principle nothing changes: the model simply gets one more term, e.g. \(y \sim \mathrm{NegBin}(\mu = e^{a + b x_1 + c x_2}, \theta)\), and glm() estimates all coefficients by maximum likelihood. In practice, four complications arise:

  1. Visualising the data and the model, because a figure has only two axes. The usual solutions are panel plots (one panel per level of a second predictor), all-in-one plots with different symbols or colours per group, and for two continuous predictors effect plots or image/contour plots of the fitted surface.
  2. Interactions. Two predictors interact when the effect of one depends on the value of the other. In a figure this shows up as lines with different slopes; in the model as a product term x1:x2 (R’s x1 * x2 means x1 + x2 + x1:x2). The coefficient of the interaction is the change in slope of one predictor per unit of the other. If an interaction is in the model, its main effects must stay in the model, even if they are not significant (the marginality principle, Nelder 1977).
  3. Collinearity. Predictors that are strongly correlated with each other (\(|r| > 0.7\), Dormann et al. 2013) cannot be separated by the model: their coefficients become unstable and their standard errors inflate (quantified by the variance inflation factor, VIF, which should stay below 10). The remedy is to check correlations before modelling and keep only one predictor of each correlated group.
  4. Model selection. With several predictors and their interactions, many candidate models exist. The full model is compared with simpler models by likelihood-ratio tests (anova()) or by information criteria (AIC, BIC; BIC penalises complexity more strongly and prefers simpler models). Backward stepwise selection (step()) drops one term at a time; best-subset selection (MuMIn::dredge()) fits all sub-models and ranks them. Note that the anova() table of a single model is sequential: each term is tested after the terms above it, so the order of the terms matters when predictors are correlated.

This practical is different from the previous ones: you do not have to write any R code (occasionally you might use R to calculate coefficient-backtransforms). All output and figures are produced for you, and the code that produced them is shown so that you can see how it was done. Your task is to read and interpret the output, exactly as you would when reading a paper or a report. Write down your answers in a text document.

The data come from the qsbsdata package; in addition MASS (negative binomial GLM), car (variance inflation factors) and MuMIn (best-subset regression) are used.
