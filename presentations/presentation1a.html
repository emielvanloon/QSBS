Note

Three real data sets from the MASS package run through the whole talk: geyser (Old Faithful eruptions), immer (barley yields) and nlschools (Dutch primary-school language scores).

A data point is one realisation of many possible ones

Dormann’s opening question: what is the diameter of an ash tree?

We measure one tree at 37 cm. Must it be this thick? Of course not — a bit more light, a bit more fertiliser, and it would differ. But it could not be any thickness either: never 4 m.

A random variable is a function producing somewhat unpredictable values within limits. What we actually see are its realisations (= observations).

A sample is a group of such realisations.

Our three samples

The histogram: bins and counts

Values are grouped into equally wide bins; the bar height is the number of observations in that bin. The bin width is a choice — and it changes what you see.

Too few bins: the shape disappears

With three bins every pupil falls into one of three blocks: the peak, the tail and the asymmetry are all gone. Note that breaks is only a suggestion — R picks nice round limits, so breaks = 8 already yields ten bins, enough for the left tail to reappear.

Too many bins: the shape drowns in noise

With 150 bins most bins hold 0 or 1 pupil and we are back to reading raw data. R’s default (Sturges’ rule) is usually a sensible starting point.

Six groups, ten pupils each

Dormann sends 10 groups of students out to measure 10 ash trees each. Here: six random samples of n = 10 pupils from the same nlschools population.

Only with many data points does a sample reveal the underlying distribution.

We rarely care about the sample itself — we use it to estimate a feature of the population we could never measure completely.

Centrality: mean, median, mode

The median is the middlemost value — the 0.5-quantile. The mode is the most frequent value.

Skew pulls the mean away from the median

geyser$duration is strongly bimodal — no single number is a good summary. Always look at the picture before trusting the mean.

Spread: the standard deviation

For roughly normal data about 68% of observations lie within ±1 s of the mean, and about 95% within ±2 s.

Here 71% and 94% of the pupils fall in the two bands. The variance is simply s squared — same information, but in squared units.

The coefficient of variation makes spreads comparable

s divided by the mean — dimensionless, so it can be compared across data sets measured on completely different scales.

n mean sd CV
geyser waiting [min] 299 72.314 13.890 0.192
immer Y1 [yield] 30 109.047 28.674 0.263
nlschools IQ 2287 11.834 2.069 0.175
nlschools lang 2287 40.935 9.004 0.220

CV < 0.05 means high precision; CV > 0.2 means low precision. nlschools$lang varies much more relative to its mean than immer$Y1 does.

Standard error ≠ standard deviation

The standard deviation describes the spread of the data. The standard error of the mean describes the accuracy of the mean estimate — it shrinks as the sample grows.

What a 95% confidence interval really means

Twenty groups each draw 25 pupils and compute mean ± 2 · sem.

Intervals in red miss the true mean — about one in twenty does. Not “95% of the data lie here”, but “95% of such intervals catch the true mean”.

Robust spread: IQR and MAD

The IQR is the range of the middle 50% of the data. The MAD is a robust counterpart of s. Neither is disturbed by a single wild value.

sd IQR MAD
clean 9.00 13 10.38
+ 1 typo 104.06 13 10.38

One mistyped score of 5000 among 2287 pupils inflates the standard deviation more than tenfold (9.0 → 104), while the IQR and the MAD do not move at all.

One typo, seen in the data

Top: on the scale of the real data the two samples are indistinguishable. Bottom: on the scale forced by the typo, all the real data collapse into a line.

Anatomy of a box plot

The box holds the central 50% (the IQR); the whiskers reach the most extreme value within 1.5 · IQR. Anything beyond is drawn as a point.

Box plots compare many samples at a glance

Two questions to ask: is the median centred in the box (→ symmetric), and are the whiskers equally long? Then mean ≈ median and normality is plausible.

Skew: which way does the tail point?

Negative skew (left) = long tail to the left; positive skew = tail to the right. Values near 0 mean “close to normal”, as for the verbal IQ on the right.

Kurtosis: how pointy is the peak?

A strongly negative kurtosis: instead of one sharp peak this sample is flat and two-humped — a shape no single centrality measure can capture.

Frequency versus density

Same shape, different y-axis. Density = frequency ÷ (n × bin width), so the total area of all bars equals 1. That is what makes it comparable to a probability distribution.

From a sample to a distribution

Left: a smooth, continuous picture of the sample, extending slightly beyond the observed range. Right: the geyser mean sits where almost no eruption happens — summary statistics never replace the plot.

Take-home messages

  1. Data are realisations of random variables; a sample is a window on a population we cannot fully measure.
  2. Plot first. A histogram or box plot shows range, skew, gaps and typos faster than any table of numbers.
  3. Location: mean, median, mode. They coincide for symmetric data and diverge under skew.
  4. Spread: s and variance for the data; CV to compare across scales; IQR and MAD when robustness matters.
  5. The standard error is about the mean, not the data — it shrinks with √n and defines the confidence interval.
  6. Skew and kurtosis quantify shape; near 0 means near-normal.
  7. Frequency → density (area = 1) is the bridge from a sample to a distribution — the subject of Chapter 3.
