Regression: Generalised Linear Models

Dormann (2020), Environmental Data Analysis — Chapters 7 and 8

Emiel van Loon

2026-09-15

Where we are going

In practical 2 we fitted one distribution to a whole sample. Now a parameter of that distribution is allowed to depend on a predictor. That is all a regression is.

At the end of this chapter you should…

  • see a regression as y ~ x: a response drawn from a distribution whose parameter is a function of the predictor
  • understand why the fit is done on the link scale, and be able to move between link scale and response scale (exp(), plogis())
  • read the output of summary(glm(...)): coefficients, standard errors, deviances, dispersion, AIC
  • draw a regression line with its 95 % confidence band
  • know how a factor is turned into dummy variables, and that the t-test and the χ²-test are just GLMs
  • fit a regression by hand with a likelihood function and optim() — for non-linear curves and for parameters other than the mean

Note

Illustrations use Dormann’s own examples: collared flycatchers, cherry trees, a bioassay, heights, smokers, Michaelis–Menten kinetics (vmkm) and hyena offspring. The practical uses data from the qsbsdata package.

From one distribution to a regression

25 male collared flycatchers (Ficedula albicollis): food items delivered to the nest per hour, and an “attractiveness” score of their plumage (1–5). Left: ignoring attractiveness, one Poisson with λ = 7.0 fits the counts. Right: the group means (red crosses: 4.6, 6.8, 6.0, 8.6, 8.8) rise with attractiveness — the mean is a function of the predictor. Attractive males are better fathers.

Every data point is a draw from its own distribution

Dormann’s Fig. 7.2. We write pieces ~ Pois(λ = f(attractiveness)): every observation is a random draw from a Poisson distribution, but which Poisson depends on x. For an unattractive male we expect 4–5 items but 10 is still plausible; for the most attractive males the distribution has shifted right and become more symmetric. This is the whole idea of regression — the rest is technique.

Fitting it is maximum likelihood, again

Same recipe as last week. For a candidate line, every point gets its own λi = a + b·xi; dpois(y_i, lambda_i, log = TRUE) gives the log-probability of that observation; sum over all 25 birds. Then search for the (a, b) that make the sum largest. Two parameters instead of one, so there is usually no formula — the computer searches. No least squares needed: this framework works for any distribution.

The flaw of a straight line — and the link function

Dormann’s Fig. 7.3, extended to the left. The straight (grey) line crosses zero at x = −3.74 (blue cross); beyond it the model predicts a negative number of food items, which a Poisson distribution cannot produce — R would refuse to fit. Harmless here (attractiveness is never negative), fatal for predictors that can be. The fix: fit the straight line on a transformed scale — for the Poisson, the logarithm — so that ln λ = a + b·x, and λ = ea+b·x is positive for every x. Within the data the two curves barely differ.

Four link functions

distribution data look like link fit on the link scale back to the response scale
normal measurements identity μ = a + b x nothing to do
Poisson, neg. binomial counts ≥ 0 log ln λ = a + b x exp()
binomial k out of n, 0/1 logit ln [p/(1−p)] = a + b x plogis()
gamma positive, skewed inverse 1/μ = a + b x 1/x

Dormann’s Fig. 7.4 and Table 7.1. The link function transforms the relationship, not the data: a straight line on the link scale becomes an exponential (log), a sigmoid (logit) or a hyperbola (inverse) on the response scale. A regression that is linear on the link scale and lets you choose the distribution is a generalised linear model (GLM). The inverse link is the confusing one: larger coefficients mean smaller fitted values.

Response scale or link scale?

Dormann’s Fig. 7.5. With a logarithmic y-axis the log-link model (red) becomes the straight line it really is, and the identity-link line (grey) becomes a curve. The response scale is where the data were collected — show it in a report. The link scale shows the form of the fitted model — show it to a statistician. “Linear” in generalised linear model refers to the link scale: additive terms in the parameters (even x² is allowed).

Drawing the line — and its uncertainty

Dormann’s three-step recipe: (1) seq() of new x values over the observed range — never beyond it; (2) predict(fm, newdata = ..., se.fit = TRUE); (3) lines() for the fit and for fit ± 2 SE. The blue band says where the true line lies in 95 % of repeated samples — it is not where new data points will fall; for that you must add the scatter σ of the distribution (green, the prediction interval).

Proportions: the binomial GLM

Bioassay (Dormann, Sect. 8.1): water fleas in 15 dilutions of a substance, y survivors out of n. Model: cbind(alive, dead) ~ S, family = binomial — the response is a two-column matrix, so the GLM knows that 20 of 20 carries more weight than 1 of 1 (point size ∝ n). Coefficients on the logit scale: intercept 5.70, slope −2.31 per mg/l. dose.p() (package MASS) returns the concentration at which half survive.

Link scale ⇄ response scale

The coefficients in summary() live on the link scale. To say something in the units of the data, back-transform:

example coefficient (link scale) back-transformed in words
flycatchers, log link intercept 1.47 e1.47 = 4.4 expected items at attractiveness 0
slope 0.148 e0.148 = 1.16 every step in attractiveness multiplies λ by 1.16
bioassay, logit link intercept 5.70 plogis(5.70) = 0.997 survival probability at [S] = 0
slope −2.31 e−2.31 = 0.10 every mg/l multiplies the odds of survival by 0.10

The same applies to predictions and confidence bands: predict() works on the link scale, so add ± 2 SE first, back-transform afterwards:

pr <- predict(fm, newdata = data.frame(S = Sn), se.fit = TRUE)
lines(Sn, plogis(pr$fit))                       # exp() for a log link
lines(Sn, plogis(pr$fit + 2 * pr$se.fit), lty = 2)
lines(Sn, plogis(pr$fit - 2 * pr$se.fit), lty = 2)

A band that is symmetric on the link scale becomes asymmetric on the response scale — and can never cross 0 or 1. That is a feature, not a bug.

A categorical predictor: height and sex

Dormann’s Figs. 7.6–7.7 (data simulated to his summary statistics). Behind the scenes R recodes the factor as 0/1, alphabetically: Female = 0 is the reference level. The intercept (173.1) is then the mean of the reference group and the “slope” sexMale (12.9) is the difference between the groups: males average 173.1 + 12.9 = 186.0 cm. The test of sexMale against 0 is the test for a sex difference — the classic t-test is nothing but this regression.

The null model, and what a predictor buys us

fm0 <- glm(height ~ 1,   data = hgt, family = gaussian)   # intercept only
fm1 <- glm(height ~ sex, data = hgt, family = gaussian)
rbind(`height ~ 1`   = c(coef(fm0), sexMale = NA, deviance = deviance(fm0), df = df.residual(fm0), AIC = AIC(fm0)),
      `height ~ sex` = c(coef(fm1),               deviance = deviance(fm1), df = df.residual(fm1), AIC = AIC(fm1)))
             (Intercept) sexMale deviance df      AIC
height ~ 1        179.59      NA 13191.93 99 776.0067
height ~ sex      173.14    12.9  9031.68 98 740.1201

The null model fits one grand mean (179.6) and leaves 99 residual degrees of freedom. Spending one extra parameter on sex cuts the residual deviance from 13 262 to 9 102 and the AIC by 36 — a huge improvement. That is the logic of every regression: reduce the leftover noise as much as possible with as few parameters as possible. With a normal distribution and an identity link the G in GLM is idle; lm() gives identical answers.

A factor with k levels becomes k − 1 columns of 0/1 dummies plus the intercept; you never build them yourself. Dormann’s butterflies: time until birds detect blue, red or yellow morphs. Waiting times are skewed — a gamma GLM (inverse link) or a log-normal (log(time) with gaussian) are the candidates. The intercept is blue; colourred and colouryellow are differences from blue on the link scale.

Two models for the same question — which to trust?

(Intercept) colourred colouryellow AIC deviance explained
gamma, inverse link 0.132 -0.039 0.060 329.153 0.239
log(time), gaussian 1.914 0.352 -0.463 105.915 0.271

Same data, two distributions, coefficients that look nothing alike because the link scales differ: for the gamma fit the fitted mean for blue is 1/0.11 ≈ 9 s and a larger coefficient for yellow means a shorter time; for the log-normal fit the yellow effect is negative. Both say yellow is found sooner. The AICs cannot be compared — one model is fitted to time, the other to log(time): different data. Compare instead the deviance explained, (null − residual)/null. In Dormann’s data the log-normal did better (14 % vs 9 %).

The 2 × 2 table of Chapter 5 in long format: one row per person, 0/1 smoker, sex as predictor — a Bernoulli GLM. plogis() of the intercept gives 0.316, exactly 12/38, the proportion of women who smoke; the intercept plus the sexMale effect back-transforms to 0.067 = 1/15. The difference is not significant (p = 0.088), the same conclusion as the χ²-test (p = 0.122). Correlation, t-test, χ²-test: all special cases of one model.

Dispersion: is the scatter what the distribution promises?

x SE(x) resid. dev / df AIC
Poisson 0.90 0.03 14.58 9811.60
neg. binomial 0.87 0.10 1.08 4377.43

For the normal distribution σ is estimated; for Poisson and binomial the variance is fixed by the mean, so we must check it: residual deviance / residual df should be ≈ 1. Dormann’s simulated counts (set.seed(101), Sect. 8.3) come from a negative binomial with θ = e: the Poisson ratio is ≫ 1 — overdispersion. The fitted line is the same, but the Poisson standard error is far too small and its p-values far too optimistic. glm.nb() (package MASS) estimates θ and repairs the standard errors.

Beyond glm(): a non-linear curve

Michaelis–Menten kinetics, vmkm from nlstools (Dormann, Sect. 8.2.2): v ~ N(μ = vmax·S/(KM + S) + B, σ). No link function makes this a straight line, so glm() cannot fit it — but optim() does not care about the shape of μ. Two tricks: a named parameter vector, and estimating log σ so that σ stays positive whatever the optimiser tries (a link function for σ). t = estimate/SE judges the parameters; B is not significant.

Modelling something other than the mean

estimate lower upper t
mean (both sexes) 2.04 1.86 2.25 15.03
clumping, males 0.44 0.33 0.60 -5.44
clumping, females : males 39.01 5.47 278.19 3.73

Spotted hyenas (Dormann, Sect. 8.4; simulated): 200 males and 200 females, same mean of 2 offspring — but a few males sire most of the cubs. So we model the negative binomial’s clumping parameter as a function of sex, holding the mean constant. Females are far less clumped than males (Dormann finds 13.6 vs 0.46, a factor ≈ 30). The CIs are computed on the link scale (± 2 SE) and back-transformed: hence asymmetric — and very wide for the ratio, though clearly above 1 (t ≫ 1.65). Any parameter of any distribution can be made a function of predictors — the only limits are the data and your imagination.

The same curve, several routes — not the same answer

All three fit an exponential ln μ = a + b·x, yet the slopes differ. They assume different noise: lm(log(y)) puts normal scatter on the log scale (so the noise grows with the mean and the back-transformed line sits below the mean); the log-link gaussian GLM puts constant normal scatter on the original scale; the Poisson lets the variance equal the mean. The curve is the same shape; the distribution decides which points get how much say. And remember: the AIC of lm(log(y)) is not comparable with the others.

Take-home messages

  1. A regression is a distribution whose parameter depends on a predictor: y ~ Dist(θ = f(x)). Fitting it is maximum likelihood, as before.
  2. The link function keeps fitted values legal (positive counts, probabilities in 0–1). Coefficients, predictions and standard errors live on the link scale; back-transform with exp() / plogis() — SEs before back-transforming.
  3. summary(glm()): estimate ± SE with a test against 0; null vs residual deviance for the fit; dispersion ≈ 1 or you are overdispersed (→ glm.nb()); AIC only for models fitted to the same response.
  4. A factor becomes 0/1 dummies: the intercept is the reference level, the other coefficients are differences. The t-test and χ²-test are GLMs.
  5. Draw the line over the observed range only, with its 95 % confidence band (predict(..., se.fit = TRUE)); the band is about the line, not about new data.
  6. When glm() cannot do it — non-linear curves, other distributions, a variance that changes with x — write the likelihood function and let optim() climb; the Hessian gives the standard errors.
