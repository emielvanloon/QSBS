Exercise 1 — Two categorical predictors

  1. Which of the four groups is the reference group that the intercept of the interaction model refers to, and why that one? Reconstruct from the four coefficients of fb_int the fitted mean telomere length of the four groups and compare with the table of group means.
  2. What does the coefficient Sexfemale:mmalinfected measure, in words? Do sex and mother’s infection status interact?
  3. Explain what the three rows of the anova() table test. Which term explains most of the deviance? What fraction of the total deviance is explained by the full interaction model?
  4. Which of the two models would you report, and why? Give the interpretation of the mmalinfected coefficient of the model you chose.
  5. Is it allowed to remove Sex from the additive model? From the interaction model? Explain the difference.

Answer

(a) The reference group is male chicks of healthy mothers: R takes the first level of each factor as the reference, and we defined the levels in the order male, female and healthy, infected. (Without the explicit levels argument R would have sorted them alphabetically, which here happens to give the same order.) All other coefficients are differences from this reference group.

The four fitted means follow by adding the coefficients that “switch on” for each group:

group coefficients added fitted mean observed mean
male, healthy intercept 0.973 0.973
female, healthy intercept + Sexfemale = 0.973 + 0.035 1.009 1.009
male, infected intercept + mmalinfected = 0.973 − 0.167 0.806 0.806
female, infected intercept + Sexfemale + mmalinfected + interaction = 0.973 + 0.035 − 0.167 − 0.018 0.823 0.823

The interaction model has four parameters for four groups, so it reproduces the four group means exactly. That is what makes it the “saturated” model for the group means; the additive model with three parameters cannot do this.

(b) Sexfemale:mmalinfected is the extra effect of an infected mother for female chicks, beyond the effect for males: it is the difference between the two slopes in the interaction plot, i.e. (female infected − female healthy) − (male infected − male healthy) = (0.823 − 1.009) − (0.806 − 0.973) = −0.186 − (−0.167) = −0.018. The estimate is tiny compared with its standard error (0.104), \(p = 0.86\), and in the interaction plot the two lines are practically parallel. There is no interaction: the mother’s infection reduces telomere length by about the same amount in sons and daughters.

(c) The table adds the terms one at a time, in the order of the formula:

  • Sex: does chick sex explain any deviance, on its own? Deviance 0.017, \(F = 0.15\), \(p = 0.70\) — no.
  • mmal: does the mother’s infection status explain additional deviance after sex has been accounted for? Deviance 1.36, \(F = 11.5\), \(p < 0.001\) — yes, by far the largest term.
  • Sex:mmal: does allowing the infection effect to differ between the sexes explain further deviance, after both main effects? Deviance 0.004, \(p = 0.86\) — no.

The full model explains \((22.086 - 20.705)/22.086 = 0.063\), i.e. about 6 % of the total deviance. Almost all of that (1.36 of 1.38) is due to the mother’s infection status. The effect is highly significant but explains little of the variation between chicks: telomere length is variable for many other reasons (brood, year, parents), which is typical of field data.

(d) The additive model. Both criteria agree: the AIC of the additive model is lower (129.9 vs 131.9, a difference of 2 units, which is exactly what one expects when a useless parameter is dropped), and the F-test of the interaction term gives \(p = 0.86\). There is no reason to keep a term that does not improve the fit and makes the model harder to read.

In the additive model mmalinfected \(= -0.175 \pm 0.052\) (\(p < 0.001\)). Interpretation: chicks of malaria-infected mothers have, at 9 days of age, telomeres that are on average 0.18 units shorter (roughly 18 % of the value for chicks of healthy mothers, 0.98), irrespective of the chick’s sex; the 95 % confidence interval for the difference is about −0.28 to −0.07.

(e) From the additive model, yes: Sex is a main effect that is part of no interaction, its coefficient is small and not significant (\(p = 0.61\)), so it can be dropped (the AIC would decrease by almost 2). Whether one wants to drop it is a different matter: if the sex of the chick was part of the design or the hypothesis, one may keep it and report that it has no effect.

From the interaction model, no. The interaction Sex:mmal contains Sex; removing the main effect while keeping the interaction violates the marginality principle (Nelder 1977; Dormann Sect. 15.4). The resulting model would be one in which the sexes have the same mean for healthy mothers by assumption but may differ for infected mothers — a strange, non-hierarchical model whose coefficients depend on the arbitrary choice of reference level. The correct order is: first test the interaction; if it can go, then look at the main effects.

  2. Write down the two fitted regression lines. What does the model assume about them?
  3. Why does the order of the terms matter in the anova() tables, and why does the residual deviance not depend on the order?
  4. What does Root:GrazingUngrazed represent? Is there evidence that the effect of root size differs between the groups? Which model would you report?
  5. Why is it important that root diameter was measured before the treatment?

Answer

(a) The two statements answer different questions. The boxplot compares the raw means of the two groups (68 vs 51 mg) and ignores everything else. But the grazed plants were, by chance, larger to begin with: their mean root diameter is 8.3 mm against 6.1 mm for the ungrazed plants, and fruit production increases steeply with root size (23.6 mg per mm). The grazed group therefore has a head start that has nothing to do with grazing. The additive model compares the two groups at the same root diameter — in the scatter plot, the open symbols lie consistently above the filled symbols for any given root size — and finds that an ungrazed plant produces 36 mg more fruit than a grazed plant of the same size.

The question “does grazing reduce fruit production?” is about the effect of grazing with everything else equal, so the model’s answer is the right one: grazing reduces fruit production by about 36 mg. The raw comparison is confounded by plant size; this is a textbook example of how a covariate can reverse the sign of a group difference (a form of Simpson’s paradox). It is also the reason why Dormann insists on plotting the data against all predictors before interpreting a group difference.

(b) With the reference level Grazed:

  • Grazed: \(\hat{y} = -127.8 + 23.56 \cdot \mathrm{Root}\)
  • Ungrazed: \(\hat{y} = (-127.8 + 36.1) + 23.56 \cdot \mathrm{Root} = -91.7 + 23.56 \cdot \mathrm{Root}\)

The additive model assumes that the two lines are parallel: the same slope for both groups, and a constant vertical distance of 36.1 mg between them. Grazing shifts the line but does not change how fruit production responds to root size. (Note the negative intercepts: they are the fitted values at Root = 0 mm, far outside the range of the data (roughly 5–10 mm), and have no biological meaning.)

(c) The anova() table is sequential: each row reports the deviance explained by a term given the terms above it. If Root and Grazing were independent (uncorrelated) in the data set, the deviance of each would be the same regardless of order. Here they are not independent — the grazed plants are bigger — so part of the deviance can be attributed to either variable:

  • Grazing entered first receives 2910: the difference between the raw group means, which is partly a size effect.
  • Grazing entered after Root receives 5264: the grazing effect at equal root size, which is larger because it is no longer masked by the size difference working in the opposite direction.

The residual deviance (1684.5 on 37 df) is the same in both tables because it belongs to the final model, which contains both terms whatever the order. Only the split of the explained deviance among the terms depends on the order. For an unbalanced or observational data set one should therefore be careful with sequential tables: the test of interest is usually the one where the term of interest is entered last, or, better, the comparison of two nested models with anova(fm_small, fm_big).

(d) Root:GrazingUngrazed is the difference in slope between ungrazed and grazed plants: the ungrazed line is estimated to be 0.76 mg per mm steeper. Its standard error (2.35) is three times the estimate, \(p = 0.75\), and the model comparison gives \(F = 0.10\), \(p = 0.75\). There is no evidence that the effect of root size differs between the groups; the parallel-lines model is adequate and should be reported. Note that in the interaction model the GrazingUngrazed coefficient (30.8, \(p = 0.076\)) is no longer “significant” — not because the grazing effect has disappeared, but because in that model it represents the difference between the groups at Root = 0, a meaningless extrapolation where the two lines are poorly determined. This is a typical side effect of including an unnecessary interaction.

(e) Because root diameter was measured before the treatment, it cannot have been influenced by grazing: it is a genuine covariate that describes the initial state of the plant, and adjusting for it makes the grazing effect cleaner. If root diameter had been measured after grazing, it might itself be a consequence of grazing (e.g. grazed plants invest in roots, or lose root mass). Adjusting for it would then remove part of the grazing effect — the part that acts through root size — and the GrazingUngrazed coefficient would only measure the residual effect not mediated by root growth. The statistical model would be the same, but the causal interpretation would be very different. Whether a variable is a covariate or an intermediate outcome is a question of design, not of statistics.

  1. Write down the fitted model on the logit scale for unsewered and for sewered areas.
  2. Calculate the fitted probability of contamination at 4 mg/l nitrate in an unsewered and in a sewered area.
  3. What do the two non-significant main effects test, and why must they stay in the model?
  4. Describe the interaction in words for a report on groundwater quality.
  5. Why an F-test rather than a \(\chi^2\) test? What would have happened to the p-value with the ordinary binomial family?

Answer

(a) The reference level of sewer is no. Hence:

  • Unsewered: \(\mathrm{logit}(p) = -3.069 + 0.066 \cdot \mathrm{nitrate}\)
  • Sewered: \(\mathrm{logit}(p) = (-3.069 - 0.133) + (0.066 + 0.554) \cdot \mathrm{nitrate} = -3.202 + 0.620 \cdot \mathrm{nitrate}\)

The seweryes coefficient shifts the intercept, the nitrate:seweryes coefficient changes the slope.

(b) At 4 mg/l:

  • Unsewered: \(\eta = -3.069 + 0.066 \times 4 = -2.80\), so \(p = e^{-2.80}/(1 + e^{-2.80}) = 0.057\), about 6 % of the wells.
  • Sewered: \(\eta = -3.202 + 0.620 \times 4 = -0.72\), so \(p = e^{-0.72}/(1 + e^{-0.72}) = 0.33\), about a third of the wells.

Both numbers can be checked against the curves in the figure at nitrate = 4.

(c) In a model with an interaction the main effects are conditional effects at the reference level or at zero of the other variable:

  • nitrate (0.066, \(p = 0.70\)) is the slope of nitrate in unsewered areas only. The test says: in unsewered areas there is no detectable relation between nitrate and contamination. That is a real and interesting result, not a statement about nitrate in general.
  • seweryes (−0.13, \(p = 0.90\)) is the difference between sewered and unsewered areas at nitrate = 0 mg/l. Nitrate concentrations of exactly 0 hardly occur, and at low nitrate the two curves are indeed close together, so this test tells us little.

Both must stay in the model because the interaction nitrate:sewer is significant and contains them (marginality). Dropping nitrate while keeping nitrate:sewer would force the unsewered slope to be exactly zero; dropping sewer would force the two curves through the same intercept. Both would be arbitrary constraints that change the meaning of the remaining coefficients and make them depend on how the factor is coded.

(d) In unsewered areas the proportion of contaminated wells is low (around 5 %) and does not increase with the nitrate concentration (the grey curve is almost flat). In sewered areas the proportion rises steeply with nitrate, from a few percent at low concentrations to about 50 % at 5 mg/l and beyond. In other words, nitrate is only a useful indicator of TCE contamination where sewers are present. This suggests that the contamination and the nitrate have a common source connected to the sewer system (leaking sewers carrying both nutrients and industrial solvents into the groundwater), rather than nitrate itself causing contamination; in unsewered areas nitrate comes from other sources (agriculture, septic tanks) that are not associated with TCE. Note that the confidence band for sewered areas at high nitrate is wide, because few areas have high nitrate.

(e) With a quasi-likelihood family the dispersion parameter is estimated from the data (\(\hat\phi = 2.1\)), and the deviance difference has to be divided by that estimate. A ratio of two estimated quantities is F-distributed, not \(\chi^2\)-distributed — exactly as in the gaussian case, where \(\sigma^2\) is estimated. The test statistic is \(F = (11.30 / 1) / 2.11 = 5.35\) on 1 and 16 df.

With the ordinary binomial family the dispersion would have been fixed at 1, the test statistic would have been the raw deviance difference 11.3 on 1 df compared with a \(\chi^2_1\) distribution, giving \(p \approx 0.0008\) — some forty times smaller. As in Practical 4b, ignoring the overdispersion would have overstated the evidence for the interaction. (Note that the dispersion in the interaction model is 2.1, much less than the 5.6 we found for the model with nitrate only: the interaction with sewerage explains part of what looked like overdispersion before.)

Answer

(a) The intercept (2.549) is the log expected richness at NAP = 0 m on a beach with exposure 8, the first factor level. The slope of NAP on the log scale per exposure class is:

  • exposure 8: \(-0.324\) (the NAP coefficient itself)
  • exposure 10: \(-0.324 - 0.338 = -0.662\)
  • exposure 11: \(-0.324 - 0.244 = -0.568\)

The interaction coefficients are the differences in slope from the exposure-8 slope. Both are negative (steeper decline on more exposed beaches) but neither is significant on its own (\(p = 0.11\) and \(0.32\)).

(b) No. The likelihood-ratio test compares the additive model (41 residual df) with the interaction model (39 residual df): LR statistic 2.28 on 2 df, \(p = 0.32\). The AIC agrees: 207.8 for the interaction model against 206.0 for the additive model — adding two parameters costs 4 AIC units and buys only 2.3 units of \(2\log L\). The test has 2 degrees of freedom because the interaction between a continuous variable and a factor with three levels adds two coefficients (one slope difference per non-reference level); anova() tests them jointly, which is the right way to judge whether “the interaction” is needed. Testing the two coefficients separately in the summary would only tell us something about each contrast with the reference level.

(c) Ranked by AIC:

model AIC ΔAIC
NAP + exposure 206.0 0
NAP * exposure 207.8 1.7
NAP only 231.7 25.7
exposure only 239.2 33.2

The additive model is clearly best; the interaction model is within 2 units (as expected when two useless parameters are added) but is not better. Of the two single-predictor models, NAP alone is better than exposure alone by 7.5 units, but both are far behind the two-predictor models: each predictor explains a different part of the variation, and both are needed. In Practical 4b we used NAP alone (AIC 231.7 for the negative binomial model) and found conspicuous residuals for exposure-8 stations; adding exposure lowers the AIC by 26 units and resolves exactly that pattern.

(d) From the additive model: exposure11 \(= -1.388\), so \(e^{-1.388} = 0.25\): at a given NAP, the expected richness on an exposure-11 beach is a quarter of that on an exposure-8 beach (or, the other way round, sheltered beaches have four times as many species). For NAP: \(e^{-0.569} = 0.57\), so every metre higher on the beach multiplies the expected richness by 0.57, i.e. a decrease of 43 % per metre, the same on all three beach types. Because the model is additive on the log scale, the effects are multiplicative on the scale of the counts.

(e) The link function is the log, so the model is linear in NAP on the log scale. On a logarithmic y-axis the fitted curves of the additive model become three parallel straight lines: same slope (the common NAP effect), shifted vertically by the exposure effects. That is exactly the “no interaction” assumption, and it is immediately visible. On a linear y-axis the same curves are exponential and not parallel — the vertical distance between them shrinks as NAP increases — which would wrongly suggest an interaction to the eye. The right-hand panel shows the interaction model, whose three lines have different slopes on the log scale; the difference from the parallel version is small, in line with the non-significant test.

Exercise 5 — Collinearity

Answer

(a) lArea and lElev, with \(r = 0.83\) — well above Dormann’s limit of 0.7 (\(r^2 = 0.69\)). The other correlations are all below 0.2. The physical reason is that the Galápagos are volcanic islands: an island is essentially a shield volcano, and a bigger volcano is both wider (larger area) and taller (higher elevation). Area and maximum elevation are two measurements of the same thing — the size of the volcano.

(b) Alone, lElev carries all the information that is correlated with island size, and since size is a strong predictor of richness, elevation “inherits” a strong effect (0.76 per log unit, \(z = 6.0\)). In the model with lArea, the coefficient of lElev measures the effect of elevation at a given area — the effect of the 31 % of the variation in elevation that is not explained by area. That residual variation is small, it does not seem to be related to richness, and the coefficient collapses to −0.07 with a standard error more than twice as large. The AIC also rises (274.0 → 275.9): elevation adds nothing once area is known.

This does not prove that elevation has no effect. It shows that, in this data set, area and elevation are so entangled that we cannot separate their effects: whatever elevation does is already captured by area. The interpretational problem of collinearity (Dormann Sect. 15.3) is exactly this: a non-significant coefficient for a collinear predictor means “no additional information”, not “no effect”.

(c) The standard error of lArea rises from 0.036 in m_area to 0.065 in m_both, a factor of 1.8. The variance inflation factor of lArea in the full model is 3.57; the VIF is the factor by which the variance of a coefficient is inflated by the correlation with the other predictors, so the standard error is inflated by \(\sqrt{3.57} = 1.89\) — which is what we observe. (A VIF of 3.6 corresponds to \(R^2 = 1 - 1/3.6 = 0.72\) of that predictor being explained by the others, consistent with \(r = 0.83\) between area and elevation.) Dormann’s threshold for action is a VIF of 10, so by that rule the collinearity is not severe enough to make the estimates unstable — but it is severe enough to make the interpretation of the two coefficients impossible, as (b) showed. The threshold of \(|r| > 0.7\) and the threshold of VIF > 10 are answers to two different questions (can I interpret the two effects separately? / are the estimates numerically unstable?).

(d) The model with lArea only (m_area): it has the lowest AIC (274.0), one parameter fewer than m_both and three fewer than m_all, and the extra predictors (lElev, lNear, lAdj) are all non-significant and change the area effect very little (0.367 → 0.389 → 0.375). m_all is 2.7 AIC units worse than m_area; lNear is the only other term with a hint of an effect (\(p = 0.07\)), and even that does not pay for itself in AIC terms.

A discussion sentence: “Plant species richness increases with island area (\(z = 0.37 \pm 0.04\), negative binomial GLM). Because area and elevation are strongly correlated across the archipelago (\(r = 0.83\)), the data cannot distinguish between an effect of area per se and an effect of elevation (or habitat diversity); we report the model with area as the single predictor, with the caveat that it should be read as an index of island size that includes elevation.”

(e) Not with these data. The colleague’s model (m_elev, AIC 294.4) is 20 units worse than the area model, so if one must choose, the data prefer area; but that preference concerns the fit, not the mechanism, and with \(r = 0.83\) neither variable can be held fixed while the other varies. What could decide the argument is data in which area and elevation are decoupled: an archipelago with flat large islands and steep small ones (e.g. atolls next to volcanic islands), or, within an island, samples of habitat diversity that vary independently of area. Alternatively, one could measure the proposed mechanism directly (the number of habitat types per island) and test whether it explains richness better than either area or elevation. In the absence of such data, the honest statement is that the two hypotheses are not distinguishable — which is itself a useful result.

  2. Does the non-significant lAREA main effect of the interaction model mean that area has no effect? What does it represent?
  3. Calculate the slope against lAREA at grazing intensity 1 and 5. Synergistic or antagonistic?

Answer

(a) In the additive model, every increase of lAREA by one unit — i.e. a tenfold increase in fragment area, because the predictor is log10(area) — raises the expected bird abundance by 6.9 birds per census, at a given grazing intensity. Every step up the grazing index (e.g. from 2 to 3) lowers abundance by 2.9 birds, at a given area. Treating GRAZE as continuous assumes that each step of the index has the same effect, which is a simplification of an ordinal scale. Both effects are highly significant, and the model assumes they simply add up.

(b) No. In the interaction model, lAREA is the slope of abundance against lAREA at GRAZE = 0, because the interaction term lAREA:GRAZE contributes nothing when GRAZE is zero. A grazing intensity of 0 does not exist — the index runs from 1 to 5 — so the coefficient is an extrapolation to a situation outside the data, with a correspondingly large standard error (2.04). The actual slopes at the grazing intensities that occur are given in (c) and are all clearly positive. This is the same phenomenon as in Exercise 2(d) and 3(c): main effects in the presence of an interaction are conditional effects at a reference point, and if that reference point is not meaningful, neither is the test. (Centring GRAZE at its mean, or at 3, would make the main effect the slope at an average grazing intensity and turn it “significant” again — without changing the model in any real sense.)

(c) Slope of abundance against lAREA \(= 2.11 + 2.14 \times \mathrm{GRAZE}\):

  • grazing 1: \(2.11 + 2.14 = 4.25\) birds per tenfold area
  • grazing 5: \(2.11 + 10.71 = 12.82\) birds per tenfold area

The interaction coefficient is positive, so the effect of area is stronger under heavy grazing: in Dormann’s terminology the two predictors act synergistically — the effect of the product term goes in the same direction as the area effect. Equivalently, the effect of grazing depends on area: the grazing slope is \(-4.55 + 2.14 \times \mathrm{lAREA}\), which is strongly negative in small fragments (−6.7 birds per grazing step at 0.1 ha) and about zero in fragments of around 100 ha (\(\mathrm{lAREA} = 2\)). In words: heavy grazing hits bird abundance hard in small fragments, whereas large fragments retain their birds even when grazed. The lines plot shows this as the three lines converging at large areas.

(d) Fitted abundance is highest in the bottom-right (large, lightly grazed fragments, > 35 birds) and lowest in the top-left (small, heavily grazed fragments), where the model even predicts negative abundance — an impossible value that signals that a gaussian model with an identity link is being extrapolated beyond its validity. Two corners of the plot contain no data and lie outside the convex hull: the top-left (there are no heavily grazed fragments smaller than about 0.5 ha) and the top-right (no heavily grazed fragments larger than about 25 ha; the large fragments in this data set are all lightly grazed). The model’s statements about those corners rest on the assumption that the linear interaction continues outside the data — pure extrapolation. The claim that “large fragments retain their birds even when grazed” is therefore only supported up to about 25 ha; for the largest fragments the effect of heavy grazing is unknown. This is the “curse of dimensionality” that Dormann discusses (Sect. 16.1.3): even though both predictors individually cover their range well, the combinations do not.

(e) Compare the interaction model with a model that allows a curved effect of area (and/or of grazing) but no interaction, e.g. ABUND ~ poly(lAREA, 2) + GRAZE, and with a model containing both, ABUND ~ poly(lAREA, 2) * GRAZE or poly(lAREA, 2) + GRAZE + lAREA:GRAZE. If the quadratic term absorbs the improvement in fit and the interaction becomes non-significant once the quadratic term is present, the “interaction” was a disguised non-linearity. If the interaction remains significant with the quadratic term in the model, it is genuine. Dormann’s advice is to include both non-linear terms and interactions in the full model and let model selection decide.

Answer

The largest correlations are between GRAZE and YR.ISOL (\(r = -0.64\): fragments isolated longer ago are more heavily grazed), between lDIST and lLDIST (0.60: the two distance measures partly describe the same isolation) and between GRAZE and lAREA (−0.56). All are below the 0.7 limit, and the VIFs are all below 2.6, far from the critical value of 10. The pair GRAZE/YR.ISOL is the one to keep an eye on: because they are correlated, the model has some difficulty attributing the effect to one or the other, which is why both have moderate p-values (0.08 and 0.11) in the full model although each is a good predictor on its own. It is not a problem for the fit, but it will show up in the model selection as an ambiguity about which of the two to keep.

(b) The p-value of an individual coefficient tests whether that predictor explains variation that the other predictors in the model do not already explain. When predictors are correlated, they share explanatory power: the model as a whole explains 68 % of the variance (\(F = 17.8\), \(p < 10^{-10}\)), but much of that is joint, and any single predictor except lAREA can be dropped with only a small loss because a correlated partner takes over its role. Two further reasons: the full model spends 7 parameters on 56 observations, so each is estimated with limited precision, and lDIST and lLDIST are simply weak predictors. “Not significant in the full model” therefore means “not needed in addition to the others”, which is a different statement from “unrelated to bird abundance”.

(c) The table shows the current model (<none>: the full model, with residual sum of squares 1996.8 and criterion value 228.3) together with every model that can be obtained by removing one term. Each row - term gives the increase in residual sum of squares (Sum of Sq) and the criterion value of the model without that term. The rows are sorted by the criterion, best (lowest) first. Rows above <none> are models that are better than the current one; rows below are worse. - lAREA is at the bottom because removing area increases the residual sum of squares by 1060 (half of what the model explains) and the criterion by 20 units: area is by far the most important predictor. - lLDIST is at the top: removing it costs only 3.8 units of RSS and lowers the criterion from 228.3 to 224.4. So lLDIST is removed in this step, and the procedure continues with the five-predictor model. (Note that R labels the column AIC even though we asked for the BIC penalty k = log(56); the values are BIC-like, which is why they differ from the AIC() values below.)

(d) Both criteria are \(-2 \log L + k \times (\text{number of parameters})\); AIC uses \(k = 2\), BIC uses \(k = \log n = \log 56 = 4.0\). BIC therefore charges twice as much per parameter, and a term has to reduce \(-2\log L\) by at least 4 (rather than 2) to earn its place. YR.ISOL reduces it by about 3.4 — enough for AIC, not for BIC — so AIC keeps lAREA + YR.ISOL + GRAZE and BIC keeps only lAREA + GRAZE. With 56 observations and moderately correlated predictors, the more conservative BIC (or the small-sample AICc) is the safer choice, following Dormann’s reasoning in Sect. 16.3.1: AIC tends to keep marginal terms when \(n\) is small. The two final models differ by one term with \(p = 0.077\); either would be defensible, and both criteria agree that lDIST, lLDIST and ALT can go.

(e) The Akaike weight of a model is \(\exp(-\Delta_i/2)\) normalised so that the weights of all 64 models sum to 1; it can be read as the probability that this model is the best of the set, given the data. A weight of only 0.23 for the top model means that the data do not single out one model: the second model (adding ALT) has weight 0.19, and seven models within \(\Delta < 2\) together carry about 1.0 of the weight — they are practically equivalent descriptions. lAREA appears in all seven models with a stable coefficient (6.9–8.2), so its importance is beyond doubt. GRAZE appears in six of the seven and YR.ISOL in five; the one model without GRAZE (model 38) has YR.ISOL with a larger coefficient, and the one without YR.ISOL is the BIC model — this is the GRAZE/YR.ISOL correlation from (a) showing up as model-selection uncertainty. ALT, lDIST and lLDIST each appear in only one or two models, with small coefficients: they are dispensable.

(f) A defensible final model is ABUND ~ lAREA + GRAZE + YR.ISOL (the AIC model, 371.1): it has the lowest AIC of all 64 sub-models and contains the three predictors that best-subset selection identifies as consistently useful. Bird abundance increases by about 7 birds per tenfold increase in fragment area, decreases by about 1.9 birds per step of grazing intensity and is higher in fragments isolated more recently (0.08 birds per year; \(p = 0.08\)). One may equally report the BIC model without YR.ISOL (ΔAIC 1.4, lower BIC), noting that the effects of grazing and isolation age cannot be fully separated because heavily grazed fragments tend to be the ones isolated longest.

The caveat: the p-values in the selected model are computed as if the model had been specified in advance, but it was chosen because its terms looked good in this data set. Selection biases the retained coefficients away from zero and makes their p-values too optimistic (Dormann Sect. 15.4; Harrell 2001). The evidence for lAREA is overwhelming and unaffected, but the modest evidence for YR.ISOL (\(p = 0.08\)) or GRAZE (\(p = 0.03\)) after selection should be read as suggestive rather than confirmatory, and ideally checked on independent data.
