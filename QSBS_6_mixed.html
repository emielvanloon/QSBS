QSBS Practical 6 — Mixed exercises

These six exercises cover the material of Practicals 1a, 1b, 2a/2b, 3, 4b and 5 together. Each exercise uses a data set from the qsbsdata package that you have not seen before; read its help page (?trunkfl etc.) before you start. Use the QSBS R command reference to look up the commands you need.

Code 
library(qsbsdata)
library(MASS)

Exercise 1 — Describing a variable

The data set trunkfl contains measurements on 619 street trees in several North American cities: the diameter at breast height (DBH, cm), the trunk flare (TF, the widening of the trunk just above the ground, in cm), the width of the boulevard strip in which the tree stands (BLVD, feet), whether the tree has damaged the sidewalk (SidewalkDamage, 0/1) and the shape of the trunk (trunk_shape).

  1. Report for TF the mean, the median, the standard deviation, the interquartile range and the skewness statistic \(\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^3/s^3\). Do the same for DBH. Which of the two variables is the more skewed, and in which direction?
  2. Make a density histogram of TF with a kernel density curve and a rug, and a boxplot next to it. Report the five-number summary from boxplot.stats() and the number and range of the boxplot outliers.
  3. Make boxplots of TF per level of trunk_shape and report the number of trees, the mean and the standard deviation per shape. Is it sensible to compare these three groups? Look carefully at the group sizes before you answer.
  4. Summarise in three or four sentences what you have learned about the distribution of trunk flare, and state which measure of location you would report in a paper and why.

Exercise 2 — Correlation and association

We stay with the street trees of trunkfl.

  1. Make a scatter plot of TF against DBH and calculate the Pearson, the Spearman and the Kendall correlation coefficient between the two variables.
  2. The Pearson coefficient (0.81) is much lower than the Spearman coefficient (0.95). Explain what this difference tells you about the shape of the relationship and about the influence of the large trees. Check your explanation by calculating the Pearson correlation between log(DBH) and log(TF) and by adding a lowess() smoother to the scatter plot of (a).
  3. Make the cross table of trunk_shape (rows) and SidewalkDamage (columns) and the table of conditional proportions of sidewalk damage per trunk shape. Which shape damages the sidewalk most often?
  4. Test the association with chisq.test(). Report the test statistic, the degrees of freedom and the p-value — and read the warning R prints. Look at the table of expected counts (chsq$expected) to see what causes it. Is the p-value trustworthy?
  5. Repeat the test after dropping the Egg trees. Does the conclusion change? Formulate the conclusion you would report, including the caveat from (d).

Exercise 3 — Fitting a distribution

HRData contains the size of the home range (HRsize) of 359 wolf-years in four study areas in western Canada, together with the pack size, a measure of primary productivity (EVIScaled) and the density of linear features (LFD). Work with the home-range size in km²:

Code 
hr <- HRData$HRsize / 1000
  1. Make a density histogram of hr and of log(hr) next to each other, each with a density curve. Calculate the skewness of both. Which distribution family does the shape of the two histograms suggest?
  2. Fit a normal, a log-normal, a gamma and a Weibull distribution to hr with fitdistr(). Collect the log-likelihood, the AIC and the BIC of the four fits in one small table. Which distribution fits best, and do the three criteria agree?
  3. Draw the four fitted densities on top of the density histogram of hr, each in its own colour, and add the empirical density curve. Where exactly do the poor fits go wrong?
  4. Plot the empirical cumulative distribution function of hr and add the cumulative distribution function of the best-fitting distribution. Then run a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test of the data against each of the four fitted distributions and report \(D\) and the p-value. Do the KS tests agree with the AIC ranking?
  5. Using the best-fitting distribution, calculate the probability that a wolf has a home range larger than 2 km², the median home-range size, and the size that is exceeded by 10 % of the wolves. Compare each of the three numbers with the corresponding value observed in the data.
  6. For the log-normal distribution the mean is \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\), which is not the same as the median \(e^{\mu}\). Calculate both from your fitted parameters, compare them with mean(hr) and median(hr), and explain in one sentence why the two differ.

Exercise 4 — A generalised linear model

elk_data records for 3848 locations whether elk were present (presence, 0/1), the distance to the nearest road (dist_roads, m), the habitat type (habitat: forest or open), the terrain ruggedness and the elevation (dem).

  1. Which distribution and which link function are appropriate for a 0/1 response? Write the model down in the notation \(y \sim \mathrm{Dist}(\ldots)\) for the model with dist_roads and habitat as additive predictors.
  2. Fit the model with glm() and report the coefficients with their standard errors. On which scale are these numbers?
  3. Back-transform the coefficients. What is the fitted probability of elk presence in forest at the road (distance 0)? By what factor do the odds of presence change per 1000 m from the nearest road, and what is the odds ratio between open and forest habitat at a given distance?
  4. Calculate the predicted probability of presence in forest at 0, 1000 and 3000 m from a road, first by hand from the coefficients and then with predict(..., type = "response").
  5. Draw the fitted curves for the two habitat types with their 95 % confidence bands, as a function of dist_roads. Remember that the standard errors come from predict(..., se.fit = TRUE) on the link scale, and that you back-transform with plogis() only after adding \(\pm 2\) SE. Add a rug of the observed distances for presences (top) and absences (bottom).
  6. Fit the model with an interaction between dist_roads and habitat and compare it with the additive model by anova(..., test = "Chisq") and by AIC. Do the two criteria agree? Which model would you report?

Exercise 5 — Regression diagnostics

In plantherb, the number of flower heads of a plant that were damaged by herbivores (Damaged) out of the number dissected (Dissected) was recorded for individual plants of four species (Species) at six locations (Location). Use only the plants with at least one dissected flower head:

Code 
ph <- plantherb[plantherb$Dissected > 0, ]
  1. Fit the binomial GLM cbind(Damaged, Dissected - Damaged) ~ Species and report the coefficients. Back-transform the intercept with plogis(): what does it mean? Compare the four fitted proportions with the average of the per-plant proportions tapply(ph$Damaged / ph$Dissected, ph$Species, mean). They are not the same — explain why.
  2. Calculate the dispersion of this model, both as residual deviance / residual df and as \(\sum r_P^2 / \mathrm{df}\). Are the data over- or underdispersed? Give a biological reason why the number of damaged heads on one plant is not a set of independent Bernoulli trials with a common probability.
  3. Refit the model with family = quasibinomial. Show that the standard errors are those of the binomial model multiplied by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\), and report what happens to the p-value of the SpeciesSV contrast. Which of the two models would you report?
  4. Calculate Cook’s distance for the binomial model, plot it and identify the most influential plant. Report its data. Why does this plant have such a large influence (look at Dissected and at the fitted value for its species)?
  5. Plot the standardised deviance residuals of the quasi-binomial model against the linear predictor and against Location. Is there structure left that Location could explain? Extend the model with Location, compare the two quasi-binomial models with anova(..., test = "F") and check whether the dispersion has improved.
  6. In two or three sentences, write the diagnostic paragraph you would put in the methods section of a paper about this analysis.

Exercise 6 — Multiple regression with two categorical predictors

bearmove contains 2768 daily movement distances of seven black bears, on the log scale (log.move), together with the season (Season: Spring, Summer, Fall), whether the observation is from the day or the night (DayNight) and the sex of the bear (Sex).

  1. Make a table of the mean log.move for the six combinations of Season and DayNight, and visualise the data: a panel plot (one panel per season, boxplots of day and night) or an interaction plot of the six group means with their standard errors. Which pattern do you see?
  2. Fit the additive gaussian model log.move ~ Season + DayNight and the model with interaction log.move ~ Season * DayNight. Report the coefficients of the interaction model. Which combination of season and day/night is the reference group?
  3. Reconstruct the six fitted group means from the coefficients of the interaction model and check them against the table from (a). How many parameters does the interaction model use for six group means?
  4. Is the interaction needed? Answer with anova() of the two models and with AIC. Why does the test have 2 degrees of freedom? Describe the interaction in words: in which season does the difference between day and night differ most from the others?
  5. The DayNightNight coefficient is positive (+0.41) although bears move less at night in two of the three seasons. Explain what this coefficient represents in the interaction model, and why its sign is not a contradiction.
  6. Add Sex to the interaction model and compare it with the model without Sex (anova(), AIC). Is the sex of the bear important? Does adding Sex change the interaction coefficients much, and what does that tell you about the relation between Sex and the other two predictors in this data set?
