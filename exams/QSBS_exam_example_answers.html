tab <- table(Swallowtail$Species, Swallowtail$Sex)

Within P. m. britannicus 40 % of the butterflies are female (55 of 138), within P. m. gorganus 36 % (20 of 56).

b (2 pt)

ch <- chisq.test(tab)
ch
## 
##  Pearson's Chi-squared test with Yates' continuity correction
## 
## data:  tab
## X-squared = 0.13987, df = 1, p-value = 0.7084
round(ch$expected, 1)
##                              
##                               female male
##   Papilio machaon britannicus   53.4 84.6
##   Papilio machaon gorganus      21.6 34.4

The expected counts under independence (53.4 / 84.6 and 21.6 / 34.4) are very close to the observed counts. The chi-square test (with continuity correction) gives \(\chi^2 = 0.14\) on 1 df, \(p = 0.71\) (without correction \(p = 0.59\)). Conclusion: there is no evidence that the sex ratio differs between the two subspecies; the small difference in the proportion of females is well within what chance would produce.

Question 4 — GLM interpretation

a (1 pt)

The response is binary (0/1), so the appropriate model is a binomial GLM with the logit link (logistic regression).

fb <- glm(RODENTSP ~ PERSHRUB, family = binomial, data = bolger)
summary(fb)$coefficients
##                Estimate Std. Error   z value    Pr(>|z|)
## (Intercept) -3.90342422  1.5577497 -2.505810 0.012217139
## PERSHRUB     0.07662335  0.0287754  2.662808 0.007749163

Fitted model: \(\mathrm{logit}(p) = \ln\frac{p}{1-p} = -3.90 + 0.0766 \cdot \mathrm{PERSHRUB}\), with \(\mathrm{RODENTSP} \sim \mathrm{Bern}(p)\); link function: logit. The slope is significantly positive (\(z = 2.66\), \(p = 0.008\)).

b (1 pt)

predict(fb, newdata = data.frame(PERSHRUB = c(20, 50, 80)), type = "response")
##          1          2          3 
## 0.08541433 0.48194368 0.90259917
exp(10 * coef(fb)["PERSHRUB"])
## PERSHRUB 
## 2.151647

Predicted probabilities of rodent presence: 0.09 at 20 % shrub cover, 0.48 at 50 % and 0.90 at 80 %. The coefficient is the change in log-odds per 1 % cover, so per 10 % cover the odds are multiplied by \(e^{10 \times 0.0766} = 2.15\): every additional 10 % of shrub cover roughly doubles the odds of native rodents being present.

c (2 pt)

xnew <- seq(0, 100, length = 100)
pr <- predict(fb, newdata = data.frame(PERSHRUB = xnew), se.fit = TRUE)     # link scale
plot(RODENTSP ~ PERSHRUB, data = bolger, pch = 16, las = 1,
     xlab = "shrub cover (%)", ylab = "native rodents present")
matlines(xnew, plogis(cbind(pr$fit, pr$fit - 2 * pr$se.fit, pr$fit + 2 * pr$se.fit)),
         lty = c(1, 2, 2), col = "black", lwd = c(2, 1, 1))

Presence of native rodents against shrub cover, with the fitted logistic curve and 95 % confidence band.

Key points: the standard errors are obtained on the link scale (se.fit = TRUE), the band is constructed as fit ± 2 SE on that scale and only then back-transformed with plogis(), so the band stays within [0, 1] and is asymmetric.

Question 5 — GLM comparison

a (1 pt)

birds$logarea <- log(birds$area)
fp <- glm(S ~ logarea, family = poisson, data = birds)
c(deviance_ratio = deviance(fp) / df.residual(fp))
## deviance_ratio 
##        1.52515
fn <- glm.nb(S ~ logarea, data = birds)
fn$theta
## [1] 47.82332

The residual deviance / df ratio of the Poisson model is 1.53, well above 1: the counts are overdispersed. A negative binomial GLM (glm.nb, \(\hat\theta = 48\)) is the appropriate refit; a quasi-Poisson model would be an acceptable alternative.

b (1 pt)

rbind(poisson = summary(fp)$coefficients["logarea", 1:2],
      negbin  = summary(fn)$coefficients["logarea", 1:2])
##          Estimate Std. Error
## poisson 0.2378341 0.01331710
## negbin  0.2463928 0.01686803

The slope estimate hardly changes (0.238 → 0.246 per unit of log area), but its standard error increases from 0.013 to 0.017. The Poisson model overstated the precision of the slope: confidence intervals should be about 25 % wider and p-values larger than the Poisson output suggests. Here the effect of area is so strong (\(z = 14.6\) even in the negative binomial model) that the conclusion — richness increases with patch area — is unaffected, but the reported uncertainty should come from the negative binomial model.

a (1 pt)

The reference level is East (alphabetically first; it does not appear among the coefficients). In Model A the age coefficient 0.0368 is the increase in maximum stem diameter per year of lifespan, the same for all aspects: a tree that lives 100 years longer reaches a diameter that is on average 3.7 cm larger.

b (1 pt)

A 200-year-old tree on a North-facing site: \(11.99 + 0.0368 \times 200 - 0.50 = 18.85\) cm.

c (1 pt)

In Model B, age (0.0399) is the slope of diameter against age for East-facing sites only (the reference level); the other slopes are obtained by adding the interaction terms (North: 0.0399 + 0.0126 = 0.0525; South: 0.0241; West: 0.0405). AspectNorth (−2.92) is the difference between North and East at age = 0, i.e. the difference between the intercepts of the two lines — an extrapolation to a tree of zero age that has no biological meaning, which is why the estimate is so uncertain (SE 3.5). In Model A, by contrast, AspectNorth (−0.50) is the difference between North and East at any age, because the lines are parallel.

d (1 pt)

No. The three interaction terms together reduce the residual sum of squares by only 39 (\(F = 0.69\) on 3 and 152 df, \(p = 0.56\)), and the AIC increases from 929.6 to 933.5 when they are added. Report Model A: diameter increases with lifespan at the same rate on all aspects, and at a given age trees on South- and West-facing sites are about 2.3 cm thinner than on East-facing sites (\(p = 0.02\) and \(0.04\)), whereas North-facing sites do not differ from East-facing ones (\(p = 0.65\)).

Question 7 — Regression diagnostics

a (1 pt)

For the straight-line model the residuals show a clear arch: they are mostly negative at the lowest fitted values (nitrogen 0), positive in the middle and negative again at the highest fitted values, and the smoother is a hump rather than a horizontal line. This systematic pattern means the straight line is the wrong functional form — the response is curved. For the quadratic model the residuals are much closer to a structureless band around 0; the smoother is nearly flat (a slight remaining hump), and the spread is similar across the fitted values. The quadratic model captures the shape adequately.

b (1 pt)

Three plots exceed the \(4/n = 0.09\) threshold (plots 8, 21 and 25, with values of about 0.22, 0.26 and 0.09), but none comes anywhere near the strict threshold of 1. Plot 21 (yield 47, the lowest of all) and plot 25 (yield 66) are the two low outliers in the nitrogen = 0 group, and plot 8 is the high-yield plot (156) at 300 lb/acre — the latter sits at the end of the x-range, where it has the most leverage on the downward bend of the parabola. Removing plots 21 and 25 would raise the curve slightly at nitrogen = 0 and reduce the curvature a little; removing plot 8 would make the decline beyond 200 lb/acre steeper. None of them would remove the curvature itself, which is carried by all 44 plots: the levelling-off between 100 and 200 lb/acre is visible in every dose group. So the conclusion about the shape (curved, saturating) is robust.

c (1 pt)

Any one of the following (with what to look for):

  • a plot of the standardised residuals against the predictor (nitrogen dose), or a boxplot of the residuals per dose group, to check for constant variance — the desired pattern is an equal spread at every dose; here the spread looks larger at nitrogen = 0;
  • a normal QQ-plot of the standardised residuals — the desired pattern is points along the straight line, which justifies the gaussian assumption behind the F-test and the confidence band;
  • a comparison with an alternative functional form, e.g. a saturating (Michaelis–Menten or log) model, which does not predict a decline at high doses — the desired outcome is that the quadratic is not clearly beaten (AIC) and that the extrapolation beyond 200 lb/acre is not driving the fit;
  • a refit without the three influential plots — the desired outcome is that the coefficients and the conclusions do not change substantially.
